Kit (the platform formerly known as ConvertKit) markets itself specifically to creators — writers, newsletter operators, course sellers — while GetResponse pitches itself as a broader marketing suite that happens to work for creators too. That difference in positioning shows up clearly once you compare what's actually in each pricing tier.

Also note Kit's per-tier prices above are anchored to a 1,000-subscriber list; like most ESPs, Kit's price rises as your subscriber count grows past that, so the $33/$66 figures are a starting point, not a ceiling.

The free plan gap is the headline difference

This is the single biggest practical difference between these two products: Kit gives you a genuinely free plan for up to 10,000 subscribers, per its pricing page. GetResponse has no permanent free plan at all — just a 14-day trial, after which you're either paying or working with a stripped-down free account (limited landing page traffic, capped webinar attendees, and GetResponse branding on messages, per its pricing page).

For a creator starting from zero — say, launching a newsletter with no existing audience — Kit's free tier means you can build a list to a meaningful size (10,000 subscribers is enough for many creators to reach a first monetization milestone) before paying anything. That's a genuinely different economic proposition than GetResponse's trial-then-pay model, and it's likely the deciding factor for creators bootstrapping from nothing.

The trade-off, per Kit's own tier breakdown: the free plan doesn't include automations at all — that's the marquee feature you unlock starting at the Creator paid tier ($33/mo, per Kit's pricing page, for "Unlimited Visual Automations"). So free-tier Kit users can send broadcasts and build an audience, but can't build the automated welcome sequences and tagging workflows Kit is known for until they upgrade.

Automation depth

Both platforms treat automation as their core value-add once you're on a paid tier, but they're built differently. GetResponse's Starter tier includes just one custom workflow — automation is intentionally gated to push you toward Marketer or Creator tiers for anything more than a single flow. Kit's automation, once unlocked on Creator or Pro, is unlimited from the entry paid tier — you're not paying more to unlock additional automation workflows, only for more subscribers, more users, or Pro-tier analytics features like Subscriber Signals and engagement analytics (per Kit's pricing page).

If your priority is building several distinct automated sequences (a welcome series, a re-engagement flow, a product launch sequence) without hitting a workflow-count ceiling, Kit's model is more straightforward: pay for the tier, get unlimited automations at that tier. GetResponse asks you to pay for the tier that unlocks unlimited automations in the first place — Starter's single-workflow cap is a real constraint if automation is central to your plan.

Creator-specific commerce features

This is where Kit's positioning as a creator-first tool is most visible. Kit's pricing page lists "Sell digital products & subscriptions" and "Paid newsletter" capability across every tier, including the free plan — meaning even a creator on the free plan can sell a digital product or run a paid subscription newsletter through Kit natively.

GetResponse doesn't advertise equivalent native paid-newsletter or creator-commerce tooling on its pricing page in the same terms; its commerce-adjacent features lean more toward webinars (available from Creator tier up, capped at 100 attendees) and general marketing automation rather than the subscription/paid-newsletter model Kit is built around. If your business model is "sell access to my writing or a digital product directly," Kit's tooling is more purpose-built for that; GetResponse's strengths sit elsewhere.

Where each one falls short

Kit's downsides: no automation at all on the free tier is a real gap — a creator who wants tag-based automated welcome emails has to pay from day one, even if their list is small. Pricing also rises with subscriber count past 1,000, so the $33/$66 figures quoted above aren't what a 5,000-subscriber creator will pay — check Kit's slider-based pricing page for your actual list size before committing. Kit also doesn't offer webinars or the broader marketing-suite tools (landing page analytics at GetResponse's depth, SMS, etc.) that GetResponse bundles in.

GetResponse's downsides: the lack of any permanent free plan is a hard blocker for a creator with zero budget and zero list — you either pay within 14 days or drop to a limited free account with branding and capped features. The single-workflow limit on Starter makes the entry tier less useful for automation-focused creators than the price alone suggests. And GetResponse isn't built around the paid-newsletter/subscription-commerce model the way Kit is — you'd be assembling that workflow yourself rather than using a feature designed for it.

Choose Kit if:

  • You're starting a newsletter or creator business from zero and want a genuinely free plan while you build your list.
  • Selling digital products, courses, or a paid newsletter directly to subscribers is core to your business model.
  • You want automation to be unlimited as soon as you pay for it, without a workflow-count ceiling to manage.

Team size and collaboration

Users matter more than it might first appear if you're running a creator business with a virtual assistant or co-founder rather than working entirely solo. Kit's tier structure ties user seats to plan level: the free plan is 1 user, Creator adds a second seat, and Pro moves to unlimited users, per Kit's pricing page. GetResponse's pricing page doesn't break out user-seat limits by tier in the same explicit way for its lower tiers — team and account-access features are more associated with its Enterprise tier, alongside SSO. If you know you'll need multiple people with login access to your email platform before you need unlimited automation, that's worth weighing: Kit makes multi-user access a mid-tier feature (Pro), while broader team controls on GetResponse are pushed to custom-priced Enterprise.

Support quality is a vendor claim, not something we tested

Both companies describe their support offerings on their pricing pages — Kit lists "24/7 email & chat support" from Creator tier up, with a priority tier at Pro; GetResponse doesn't call out support level explicitly below Enterprise, where "dedicated support" is listed as an Enterprise-exclusive feature. Neither of these is something we've tested directly, and support quality is notoriously hard to evaluate from a pricing page alone — response times, staff expertise, and time-zone coverage all vary in practice. Treat any support claim here as the vendor's own description, not a verified service-level guarantee, and if support responsiveness matters to your decision, it's worth asking each vendor's sales or support team directly before committing.

Choose GetResponse if:

  • You want webinars bundled into your email platform rather than paying for a separate webinar tool.
  • Your priority is broader marketing infrastructure (landing pages, broader automation types, eventual SMS/transactional email on Enterprise) rather than creator-commerce specifically.
  • You're fine starting on a trial and moving straight to paid, and don't need a permanent free tier.

The bottom line

For most solo creators and newsletter writers, Kit's free plan and creator-commerce focus make it the more natural starting point — you can build an audience and even monetize before paying anything, provided you can live without automation until you upgrade. GetResponse makes more sense if you're a creator who also wants webinars and broader marketing tooling and doesn't mind paying from the outset. Confirm current subscriber-tier pricing for your specific list size on both vendors' pricing pages, since both scale price with subscriber count past the entry tier shown here.
