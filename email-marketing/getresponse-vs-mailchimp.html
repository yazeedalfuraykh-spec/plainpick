GetResponse and Mailchimp show up on almost every "best email marketing tool" list, and for good reason — both are mature platforms with over a decade of development behind them. But they're built around different assumptions about who's using them, and that shows up in pricing, free-tier limits, and what you get once you outgrow the basics. Here's what's actually true, sourced directly from each vendor's pricing page, not marketing copy.

The short version

If you want a genuinely free plan to start on and don't mind a smaller free-tier email allowance, Mailchimp's free plan is the more useful option for a brand-new list. If you're planning to use webinars, landing pages, or heavier automation from day one and are comfortable starting on a trial rather than a permanent free plan, GetResponse's paid tiers pack in more marketing tooling per dollar.

A few things worth flagging as analysis rather than fact: Mailchimp's Essentials, Standard, and Premium prices above are promotional rates for the first 12 months per its pricing page — the renewal price is higher, so budget for that before committing to a year of use. GetResponse's entry Starter plan is billed in a currency tied to your location; US visitors may see a different number, so verify on the page before buying. Neither vendor publishes a "forever" discount — read the fine print on any deal before you commit annually.

What each free/trial tier actually gives you

Mailchimp's free plan is a real free plan: no expiration, capped at 250 contacts and 500 sends a month (or 250 a day), with one audience and one seat. It's genuinely usable for a hobby list or early-stage newsletter, just with basic templates and no scheduling automation beyond what's built in.

GetResponse doesn't have a permanent free plan — it's a 14-day trial with premium features unlocked, then a stripped-down free account afterward with restricted functionality (per GetResponse's pricing page: webinars capped at 10 participants during trial, landing pages capped at 1,000 unique visitors/month, and GetResponse branding on messages that's only removable on a paid plan). If your workflow depends on having a stable no-cost plan indefinitely — say, you're testing an idea with no budget — Mailchimp's free tier is the safer starting point. If you want to fully trial a broader feature set (webinars, dedicated automation builder, AI content tools) before paying, GetResponse's trial gives you more to test, just on a clock.

Automation and marketing tools

This is where the platforms diverge most. GetResponse's Starter plan includes just one custom automation workflow, per its pricing page — you need to move to Marketer or above for unlimited workflows. Mailchimp's Standard plan lists up to 200 marketing automation flows (with up to 4 steps supported even on Essentials), and generative AI tools are called out as included at the Standard tier.

GetResponse's differentiator is baked-in webinar hosting, which Mailchimp doesn't offer at all: Creator-tier and above support webinars for up to 100 attendees (GetResponse states 3-hour recording storage), scaling to 1,000 attendees on Enterprise. If webinars are part of how you nurture a list, that's a real capability gap on Mailchimp's side — you'd need a separate tool (Zoom, Livestorm, etc.) layered into your email workflow.

GetResponse also bundles landing pages with unlimited page creation across all its paid tiers, whereas Mailchimp's page-building tools are present but positioned more as a secondary feature than a core pillar, per its own marketing.

Where each one falls short

GetResponse's downsides: no permanent free plan is a real barrier for anyone testing on zero budget. The entry Starter tier's single automation workflow is thin if automation is the reason you're shopping for a platform — you'll likely need Marketer or Creator to get real value, which roughly triples or quadruples the entry price. Support and advanced features (SMS, transactional email, SSO) are gated behind Enterprise, meaning mid-size teams that need any one of those specific things get pushed into custom-pricing territory.

Mailchimp's downsides: the advertised prices on Essentials, Standard, and Premium are promotional and step up after 12 months — this is a genuinely common complaint about Mailchimp's pricing transparency, and you should model your year-two cost before signing up. The free plan's 500-emails-per-month cap is tight even for a small list that emails somewhat regularly (a weekly newsletter to 250 people alone would burn through a meaningful chunk of that). Mailchimp doesn't offer webinars natively, and users report (this is a well-known industry pattern rather than something we've verified ourselves) that per-contact pricing scales quickly as your list grows into the tens of thousands.

Choose GetResponse if:

  • You want webinars, landing pages, and automation bundled into one tool instead of stitching together separate subscriptions.
  • You're comfortable starting on a trial rather than needing an indefinite free plan.
  • Your list is under a few thousand contacts and you want unlimited sends without worrying about a monthly send cap.

Choose Mailchimp if:

  • You need an actually-free plan with no time limit, even if the send allowance is small.
  • You're already inside the broader Intuit ecosystem (Mailchimp is owned by Intuit) and want integration with QuickBooks or other Intuit tools.
  • You want a platform with a long enterprise track record and don't mind the promotional-pricing-then-step-up billing model.

A word on list size

For a 2,000-subscriber list, the free-vs-trial distinction matters less than it seems — you'll likely be in a paid tier on either platform within a few months regardless, since both free/trial tiers are sized for lists well under 1,000 contacts. What matters more at that size is whether you need webinars or heavier automation (lean GetResponse) or whether you want the lower headline price and don't mind renegotiating after year one (Mailchimp, but budget for the renewal price, not the promotional one).

For a list under 500 contacts that emails infrequently, Mailchimp's free plan is very likely the cheaper real-world option — you may never need to pay at all.

The bottom line

Neither platform is objectively "better" — they're optimized for different buyers. GetResponse is built for people who want marketing infrastructure (webinars, automation, landing pages) in one subscription and don't mind paying from the start. Mailchimp is built for people who want to start free and are comfortable with the platform's promotional pricing structure once they scale up. Check both pricing pages directly before you commit, since promotional rates and regional currency display can change what you actually pay.
