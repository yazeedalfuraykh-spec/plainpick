"Full coverage" is one of the most misleading phrases in car rental. It gets used by counter staff, booking platforms, and third-party insurers to mean three different things, and only one of those actually means what a traveler assumes it means. This piece breaks down the terms so you can tell which one you are actually buying.

What CDW Actually Is

Fact, from Rentalcars.com's own stated definition: a Collision Damage Waiver (CDW) is "a type of cover that limits your liability for damage to certain parts of a rental car."

Read that definition literally and it already tells you the two things most travelers miss:

  1. It limits liability. It does not remove it. CDW caps what you owe, up to an excess amount, rather than reducing your exposure to zero.
  2. It covers "certain parts" of the car. It is not automatically whole-vehicle coverage. Which parts are included and excluded is set by the individual rental company's terms, not by a universal standard.

This is why two rental companies can both advertise "CDW included" and mean meaningfully different things. The word CDW describes a category of product, not a fixed contract.

Excess: The Number That Actually Matters

Excess (also called a deductible in North American terms) is the amount you are responsible for paying out of pocket if the car is damaged, before CDW coverage takes over for the rest. If your excess is a given amount and repair costs come in below that amount, you pay the full repair cost yourself; CDW only starts covering costs above the excess threshold.

Analysis, not a single cited source: this is the standard structure across the rental industry globally, though the exact excess amount, and whether it is fixed or a percentage of the vehicle's value, varies by rental company, country, and vehicle class. No single verified figure applies across the industry, so treat any specific excess amount you see quoted elsewhere as one company's current terms, not a general rule.

Two products change what happens to that excess amount:

  • Zero-excess or "super CDW" add-ons, sold by the rental company itself, reduce your excess exposure to zero (or close to it) in exchange for a daily fee added to the rental cost.
  • Standalone excess insurance, sold by third-party insurers rather than the rental company, reimburses you after the fact for excess charges the rental company has already taken from your deposit. This is typically cheaper than the rental counter's own zero-excess product, but it works differently: you still pay the excess at the counter first, then claim it back from the insurer afterward, rather than avoiding the charge entirely at pickup.

Deposit vs Excess vs CDW: Three Different Things

These three terms get used interchangeably by travelers, and they are not the same thing:

  • Deposit is a hold placed on your credit card at pickup, refunded (fully or partially) after the return inspection confirms no damage, unpaid tolls, or outstanding fines. It is a security mechanism, not an insurance product.
  • Excess is the maximum amount you are financially responsible for if damage occurs, as described above.
  • CDW is the coverage that pays for damage above the excess amount, up to whatever limits and exclusions the rental company's specific policy sets.

A rental company can set a deposit that is larger than your excess (common practice, since the deposit also covers potential tolls and fines, not just collision risk), which is why "my deposit was higher than the excess I was quoted" is not a contradiction. It is two separate numbers doing two separate jobs.

What "Full Coverage" Usually Still Excludes

This is the section that matters most and where honesty pays off for the reader. Rental industry practice, reported consistently across booking platforms and rental company FAQs though not confirmed against a single named company's current published terms for this article, commonly excludes the following from CDW or "full coverage" packages even when zero-excess add-ons are purchased:

  • Tires and wheels. Puncture, sidewall, or rim damage from potholes, curbs, or off-road driving is frequently excluded, on the reasoning that it is treated as wear-and-tear or driver-caused rather than collision damage.
  • Windshield and glass. Chips and cracks from road debris are commonly carved out into a separate, sometimes optional, glass coverage add-on.
  • Undercarriage damage. Scraping the underside of the vehicle, common on unpaved or mountain roads, is a frequent exclusion, which matters directly for the kind of road conditions described in our Georgia rental guide.
  • Interior damage and cleaning fees. Stains, smoke odor, and excessive cleaning needs are typically billed separately from collision coverage.
  • Keys. Lost or locked-in keys often carry a flat replacement or lockout fee outside the waiver.
  • Towing and roadside recovery, in some policies, particularly for single-vehicle incidents (running off the road with no other party involved) rather than collisions with another vehicle.

Vendor claim, common industry language: the term "full coverage" itself is rarely a defined legal term in a rental contract; it is marketing shorthand used inconsistently between companies. The contract document you actually sign will use CDW, excess, and named exclusions, not "full coverage," which is why reading the actual terms rather than the marketing label is the only reliable way to know what you bought.

A Practical Way to Decide

  1. Find the excess amount in writing, not just "CDW included," before you compare rental offers on price. A cheaper daily rate with a high excess can cost more overall if anything happens.
  2. Check the exclusions list directly with the rental company, especially tires, glass, and undercarriage if your trip involves rough or mountain roads.
  3. Compare the rental counter's zero-excess product against a standalone excess insurance policy bought separately before you travel. The standalone option is often cheaper, but remember it works as a claim-back, not a point-of-sale waiver, so you need to be able to front the excess amount on your card at pickup either way.
  4. Photograph the car at pickup and return, every angle, regardless of which coverage you bought. Documentation is the actual thing that resolves disputes, not the coverage tier's name.
  5. Ask what happens to your deposit if a toll or fine posts after you have already returned the car. This is a common real-world scenario that neither "CDW" nor "excess" as terms directly address, and rental companies handle it differently.

None of this requires the most expensive option at the counter to be the right one. It requires knowing which number (excess) and which list (exclusions) actually determine your exposure, since those are the two things "full coverage" as a phrase does not reliably tell you.
